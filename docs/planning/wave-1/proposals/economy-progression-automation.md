# Economy, Progression, Automation, and Sandbox Viability — Wave 1 Proposal

**Mission:** `W1-DES-02`  
**State:** PROPOSAL / NON-CANONICAL  
**Required review:** `W1-REV-GAME`

## Review Index

- **EPA-D1 — Economy as exchange network (§8):** model currencies/resources, time, attention, risk, access, relationships, and capability as interacting sources/sinks/transformations rather than one money-per-minute ladder.
- **EPA-D2 — Progression as capability graph (§9):** progression unlocks verbs, access, knowledge, leverage, scale, relationships, and world influence; numeric growth is supporting evidence, not the whole progression model.
- **EPA-D3 — Lifestyle viability (§10):** a soft specialization is viable when it can sustain goals/foundational needs via its own outputs, trade, services, substitutions, or optional cross-system participation without constant mandatory convergence.
- **EPA-D4 — Automation economics (§11):** automation trades recurring labor/attention for setup capital, infrastructure, knowledge, space, maintenance, logistics, or other constraints and must unlock higher-order decisions after burden falls.
- **EPA-D5 — Dominant-strategy resistance (§12):** evaluate dominance by goal coverage, opportunity cost, switching/friction, risk, and cross-system externalities using optimizer/exploit cohorts; do not force equality by a scalar reward rate.
- **EPA-D6 — Late-game sink portfolio (§13):** use several optional sink/ambition families so accumulated wealth/resources remain strategically useful without one exponential price wall or mandatory industrial path.
- **EPA-D7 — Gate and catch-up policy (§14):** gates are legible and purpose-bound; multiple paths/substitutions are preferred for foundational needs; switching/hybridization must avoid punitive permanent obsolescence unless explicitly intended.
- **EPA-D8 — Simulation contract (§15):** versioned economy/progression simulations bind rules, content, persona, seeds, time horizon, goal policy, and output distributions; simulations test viability/dominance/inflation/automation but do not replace player-surface evaluation.
- **Evidence (§5):** project mandates call for radical sandbox viability, automation as a progression axis and sink, multiple lifestyles, dominant-strategy detection, economy simulations, durable late-game ambition, and resistance to one scalar quality target.
- **Experiments (§18):** source/sink balance, persona viability, dominant-route exploit search, automation payback/burden, switching, inflation/sink, progression reachability, and perturbation/sensitivity runs are required before balance claims become canonical.
- **Reviewer attack points:** one universal currency dominating all values; hidden mandatory farming/automation; automation payback trivially optimal or never rational; late-game inflation solved only by arbitrary price multipliers; catch-up exploits; dead-end gates; simulation personas encoding the desired answer; balance robustness collapsing under small parameter changes.

## 1. Objective

Define a testable economy/progression/automation foundation that can support several viable ways to live in Everfield, preserve meaningful resource/time/attention choices across long play, and make automation a progression in leverage rather than either a mandatory optimizer path or a passive end-state.

The proposal specifies economy and progression structures, lifestyle viability, automation economics, dominant-strategy detection, late-game sinks, gating/switching principles, simulation outputs, observability, failure modes, and bounded experiments. It does not invent balance values.

## 2. Scope

In scope:

- economic value dimensions and source/sink/transformation modeling;
- progression capability/access/knowledge graph;
- lifestyle/soft-specialization viability;
- resource substitution/trade/service principles;
- automation setup/operation/payback/leverage model;
- time/attention and burden economics;
- dominant-strategy and exploit detection;
- late-game inflation/sink/ambition principles;
- gate visibility, switching, catch-up, and recovery;
- progression reachability and circular-dependency detection;
- simulation personas, workloads, outputs, and sensitivity analysis;
- observability/evidence and reopen conditions.

## 3. Non-goals

This proposal does **not**:

- set prices, yields, wages, XP curves, stamina costs, drop rates, machine costs, payback periods, season lengths, or concrete thresholds;
- define final currencies/resources or guarantee a particular number of them;
- require all lifestyles to generate equal money or advance at equal speed;
- choose the final automation fiction or power/maintenance model;
- decide the final skill tree or unlock catalog;
- guarantee that every content object is economically tradable;
- solve subjective fun with simulation;
- authorize gameplay implementation.

## 4. Intent constraints and assumptions

### 4.1 Observed intent constraints

The authoritative packet directs that:

1. Everfield supports several overlapping lifestyles rather than one canonical playthrough.
2. Automation is a central progression axis, normally exchanging higher setup/capital cost for lower recurring labor.
3. Automation should release attention for higher-level ambition rather than remove gameplay.
4. Late-game currency/resource accumulation needs durable sinks beyond automation alone.
5. Different lifestyles need evidence of viability and distinctness rather than nominal labels.
6. Dominant strategies, unreachable progression states, circular unlock dependencies, inflation, and automation payback should be detected through simulation/exploit search.
7. Evaluation should use several personas/signals rather than one optimizer or one `fun_score`.
8. Narrative/social/exploration/progression value may matter even when not reducible to sale price.
9. Exact balance remains an empirical design task, not a current fact.

### 4.2 Assumptions to test

- Foundational needs can be made reachable through several direct or exchange paths without making every system interchangeable.
- Time and attention can act as meaningful economic constraints even when the player becomes wealthy.
- Automation can be rational at some scales/goals without becoming universally optimal immediately.
- Several sink families can sustain late-game choice better than merely multiplying prices.
- Lifestyle viability can be measured with a vector of outcomes/goals rather than a single wealth metric.
- Progression switching/catch-up can preserve identity and opportunity cost without punishing experimentation excessively.
- Abstract/batch simulation can identify obvious dominance and dead ends before detailed player-facing balancing, provided its limitations are explicit.

## 5. Evidence, inference, recommendation

### 5.1 Evidence

The charter/game mandate require:

- manual labor → tools → infrastructure → partial automation → production/logistics → higher-level operation as a directional progression hypothesis;
- meaningful optional lifestyles and hybrid play;
- durable late-game uses for capital/resources;
- broad but coherent systems rather than catalog growth;
- automation payback and economy simulation as research questions;
- exploit/dominant-strategy detection and progression reachability as evaluation obligations.

The evaluation seed calls for synthetic player populations, economy simulations, strategy-diversity/dominance signals, progression velocity, burden, automation payback, and path diversity without turning them into one scalar acceptance score.

### 5.2 Inference

Money alone cannot be the canonical measure of viability. A social/exploration/quest-focused route can deliver access, knowledge, relationships, unique resources, world-state changes, or services that are valuable even if direct cash output is lower.

Likewise, an automation design cannot be judged only by a static payback ratio. It changes attention availability, scale, risk, logistics, resilience, opportunity cost, and access to new goals.

### 5.3 Recommendation

Adopt the structures below as candidate design contracts and require the simulations/experiments before exact economy/progression values become canonical.

## 6. Alternatives considered

### A. One universal currency as the primary progression metric — reject as target

Simple and legible, but encourages every activity to collapse into currency/hour and lets the best farming strategy buy every other system. A general currency may still exist; meaningful nonfungible access, knowledge, relationships, capabilities, time, risk, and specialized resources should constrain universal conversion where it creates better choices.

### B. Fully separate system economies — reject

Prevents one route from dominating but fragments the shared world and makes lifestyle interactions meaningless. Prefer connected markets/resources/services with friction, specialization, and multiple sources.

### C. Equalize all activity rewards per unit time — reject

This creates false symmetry and Goodhart pressure. Viability should consider goal coverage, resource/access diversity, decision identity, risk, and opportunity costs.

### D. Hard-class progression trees — reject as default

They provide strong identity/balance boundaries but conflict with soft specialization/hybridization. Scoped commitments or mutually exclusive choices may exist when clearly justified.

### E. Automation has a fixed universal payback window — reject

Different systems/goals/scales should create different rationality. Exact payback is empirical and depends on attention value, risk, setup constraints, scale, and downstream options.

### F. Solve inflation with exponential price scaling — reject as sole answer

It can consume currency but may turn late game into arbitrary number inflation. Prefer several ambition/sink families with distinct benefits and constraints.

## 7. Economy vocabulary and model

### 7.1 Value dimensions

Candidate dimensions include:

- general currencies;
- domain resources/materials;
- time/calendar opportunity;
- player attention/input burden;
- space/land/capacity;
- infrastructure slots/capacity;
- knowledge/recipes/research;
- access/permissions/regions;
- relationships/social capital;
- risk/durability/recovery cost;
- energy/maintenance/logistics if selected later;
- world-state/narrative consequences;
- scarce unique/limited opportunities.

Not all require numeric “currencies.” They are constraints/values that affect choices.

### 7.2 Economic edge types

Model systems/content through edges such as:

```text
SOURCE       -> creates resource/value under conditions
SINK         -> permanently or durably consumes value
TRANSFORM    -> inputs -> outputs, including time/attention
STORE        -> delays/hedges value
SERVICE      -> exchanges value for labor/access/relief
TRADE        -> market/player exchange relationship
GATE         -> requires state/value to unlock capability/content
SUBSTITUTE   -> alternative route to equivalent need
AMPLIFY      -> improves another production/goal path
RISK         -> expected/conditional loss/recovery cost
EXTERNALITY  -> changes prices/world/social state outside local action
```

### 7.3 Source/sink identity

Every major resource should eventually have:

- meaningful sources;
- meaningful sinks or conversion roles;
- lifecycle by progression stage;
- scarcity/abundance assumptions;
- substitutes/trade paths where appropriate;
- exploit/dominance tests;
- observability in simulation.

A resource whose only long-term purpose is “sell for money” should face scrutiny unless intentionally simple.

## 8. EPA-D1 — Economy as an exchange network

### 8.1 Shared world, imperfect fungibility

The economy should connect lifestyles while preserving specialized value. A farmer, explorer, social player, crafter, merchant, or other later-defined specialization may create different value forms and exchange them through markets, services, quests, relationships, production, or direct use.

### 8.2 General currency

If a general currency exists, it should be powerful enough to support trade/convenience but not necessarily able to bypass every progression/relationship/knowledge/content gate.

**Risk:** universal convertibility can make the highest cash-per-time route dominate the whole game.

### 8.3 Time and attention

Track player time/attention as real opportunity costs:

- active inputs;
- travel;
- maintenance;
- supervision;
- configuration;
- planning;
- waiting/calendar time;
- opportunity windows.

Automation/infrastructure changes these costs and therefore changes the economy even if material output stays equal.

### 8.4 Price/value dynamics

Dynamic prices/markets are optional design choices, not assumed. If used, they need bounded behavior, observability, and anti-exploit tests. Static pricing can still support rich economy through costs, transforms, scarcity, gating, and sinks.

## 9. EPA-D2 — Progression as a capability graph

### 9.1 Progression node types

Represent progression as nodes such as:

- new verb/capability;
- efficiency/leverage improvement;
- new source/sink/market;
- new area/access route;
- new content/recipe/knowledge;
- relationship/social privilege;
- property/infrastructure scale;
- automation/logistics capability;
- narrative/world-state branch;
- recovery/risk-management capability;
- customization/expression/mastery.

### 9.2 Gate edge types

```text
REQUIRES_RESOURCE
REQUIRES_CAPABILITY
REQUIRES_KNOWLEDGE
REQUIRES_RELATIONSHIP
REQUIRES_WORLD_STATE
REQUIRES_TIME_OR_EVENT
REQUIRES_CHOICE
ALTERNATIVE_TO
SUBSTITUTES_FOR
UNLOCKS
INVALIDATES_OR_EXCLUDES
```

Exact schema belongs downstream.

### 9.3 Reachability invariant

For every required progression target, later validators should prove or search that at least one legal path exists from supported start states under declared content/rules.

For optional goals, unreachable states may be legitimate only when conditioned on explicit choices/world branches rather than accidental circular dependencies.

### 9.4 Capability before numbers

Major progression milestones should preferably add/change decisions. Pure numerical upgrades can fill gaps and reward mastery, but a long progression chain containing only bigger outputs is weak against the project’s agency goal.

## 10. EPA-D3 — Lifestyle viability

### 10.1 Viability vector

For each candidate lifestyle/persona over a horizon measure:

- goal completion/reachability;
- foundational resource access;
- general purchasing/trade ability where relevant;
- progression capability access;
- burden/time use;
- decision/strategy diversity;
- unique strengths/resources/access;
- dependence on other systems and whether dependence is optional/substitutable;
- failure/recovery resilience;
- switching/hybridization opportunities;
- late-game ambitions available.

### 10.2 Viability does not mean equality

A high-risk explorer may be volatile but gain unique access; a social route may progress through relationships/services; a producer may create steady material wealth. Different vectors are desirable if no supposedly valid lifestyle repeatedly hits unavoidable foundational dead ends.

### 10.3 Foundational needs

Later synthesis should explicitly name foundational needs (if any) and require multiple source/service/substitution routes unless a common mechanic is intentionally part of the game’s shared foundation.

### 10.4 Trade/service bridge

A player should often be able to use strength in one system to obtain some outputs of another without performing its full low-level loop, preserving shared-world interdependence.

## 11. EPA-D4 — Automation economics

### 11.1 Cost categories

Automation may cost some mixture of:

- capital/currency;
- materials;
- knowledge/research;
- infrastructure/space;
- relationships/services;
- setup time;
- maintenance;
- energy/input resources;
- logistics capacity;
- quality/yield tradeoff;
- risk/reliability;
- opportunity cost.

No category is mandatory until later system design.

### 11.2 Benefit categories

Automation may provide:

- fewer repeated inputs;
- lower travel/supervision burden;
- higher throughput/scale;
- more consistency/timing coverage;
- new processing/logistics capabilities;
- operation across larger/multiple sites;
- resilience to missed manual schedules;
- free attention for other goals.

### 11.3 Payback model

A useful simulation concept:

```text
payback = setup_cost_vector
          versus
          avoided_recurring_costs
          + new_capability_value
          + scale_value
          + risk/reliability effects
```

Do not collapse the vector to currency unless a specific analysis intentionally monetizes components with explicit assumptions.

### 11.4 Scale curve

Manual action should often remain rational at small scale because fixed automation setup cost dominates. As scale/repetition/burden rises, automation becomes more attractive. The actual crossing points must be simulated per system.

### 11.5 Higher-order decision invariant

For each automation tier ask: **what new decisions become possible or important after the low-level burden is removed?**

If the honest answer is “none; wait for output,” the tier needs stronger interaction, new goals, or reconsideration.

### 11.6 Optionality

Automation can be highly advantageous for large operations without being mandatory for all players. Narrative/basic progression should not silently require industrial scale unless explicitly justified.

## 12. EPA-D5 — Dominant strategy and exploit resistance

### 12.1 Dominance definition

A route is suspiciously dominant when, across many reasonable goals/personas, it provides superior progress/value with insufficient opportunity cost, risk, skill requirement, access cost, or tradeoff such that ignoring it is irrational regardless of desired lifestyle.

A route can be intentionally best for its own specialization without being globally dominant.

### 12.2 Detection cohorts

Use several agent policies:

- wealth optimizer;
- fastest-progression optimizer;
- low-input/chore-averse;
- risk-averse;
- explorer/discovery-seeking;
- social/relationship-focused;
- infrastructure/automation-heavy;
- direct/manual specialist;
- hybrid opportunist;
- completionist;
- exploit/search/fuzz policy.

### 12.3 Exploit classes

Search for:

- positive loops producing unbounded value without meaningful constraint;
- buy/process/sell arbitrage;
- timing/reset/save-load exploitation;
- duplication/reference errors;
- automation loops with zero ongoing constraint where not intended;
- catch-up/refund/respec loops;
- market manipulation if dynamic pricing exists;
- progression gate bypass through general currency;
- low-risk route dominating high-risk content rewards;
- exponential compounding that makes all other content irrelevant.

### 12.4 Balance response

Do not automatically nerf the top strategy to average. Determine whether dominance comes from missing opportunity cost, broken source/sink, wrong gate, poor alternatives, exploit, or an intentionally powerful late-game reward.

## 13. EPA-D6 — Late-game sink and ambition portfolio

Candidate sink/ambition families:

- automation/infrastructure/logistics;
- property/world/community projects;
- research/knowledge/unlocking;
- customization/aesthetic/prestige;
- collection/completion/mastery;
- services/convenience/labor/delegation;
- social/faction/community investment;
- exploration/expeditions/risk;
- production-chain specialization/quality;
- rare long-horizon projects;
- maintenance/resilience where it creates decisions rather than tax chores.

### 13.1 Sink quality criteria

A durable sink should ideally:

- create or support a goal/capability/expression;
- remain optional enough to support different lifestyles;
- have understandable value;
- avoid simply deleting wealth with no decision;
- scale or diversify as the player grows;
- interact with other systems/content;
- not force infinite price inflation as the only challenge.

### 13.2 Wealth saturation

Simulation should measure how quickly common resources/currency become effectively unconstrained for representative profiles and whether available ambitions remain meaningful afterward.

## 14. EPA-D7 — Gates, switching, catch-up, recovery

### 14.1 Gate record

Later detailed design should capture:

```yaml
gate_id: <stable>
blocks_or_unlocks: <goal/capability/content>
requirements: []
reason: <design intent>
visibility: <how player learns it>
alternative_paths: []
miss_or_failure_recovery: <rule>
lifestyle_impact: []
world_branch_effect: <optional>
```

### 14.2 Avoid circular lockout

Progression graph validators should catch cycles where capability A requires B while B is reachable only after A unless a third legal source exists.

### 14.3 Switching/hybridization

Switching may have costs: learning, equipment, relationships, infrastructure, time, or opportunity loss. But experimentation should not normally create a permanently inferior account merely because early investments are sunk.

Possible catch-up tools include shared foundational capabilities, transferable resources, resale/reuse, accelerated early progression after account knowledge, service/trade bridges, or respec mechanisms. Exact choice is open.

### 14.4 Irreversible choices

Allowed for narrative/world identity or meaningful commitment when:

- consequence is reasonably signaled;
- content remains sufficient on each branch;
- no branch becomes silently nonviable;
- save/replay/verification can represent the branch state.

## 15. EPA-D8 — Simulation contract

### 15.1 Simulation identity

Every economy/progression result used as evidence should bind:

```yaml

goal_set: []
action/model assumptions: []

```

### 15.2 Core outputs

Record distributions/time series such as:

- resource/currency stocks and flows;
- source/sink contributions;
- time/attention allocation;
- progression nodes reached/time-to-reach;
- goal completions/failures;
- burden/maintenance actions;
- automation adoption/setup/payback vectors;
- trade/service usage;
- system participation;
- switching events/costs;
- dead ends/circular gates;
- strategy diversity/concentration;
- late-game sink consumption;
- exploit/anomaly flags.

### 15.3 Persona policy limits

A persona is an evaluator assumption, not ground truth about players. Version policies; run several; include exploit and noisy/nonoptimal behavior; measure sensitivity to policy changes.

### 15.4 Abstract versus executable simulation

Early planning can use graph/spreadsheet/batch models to test structure. Before canonical balance or implementation readiness, important claims should increasingly use the real/shared gameplay simulation kernel once available.

## 16. Observability and evaluation

Diagnostic vector:

- Gini/concentration-like source contribution by resource/goal where useful, not as sole target;
- fraction of foundational needs supplied through top route;
- number/diversity of viable routes to key goals;
- progression reachability/cycle failures;
- time/attention burden by lifestyle/stage;
- currency/resource velocity and stock accumulation;
- source/sink ratio by stage;
- unused/dead resource frequency;
- trade/service dependence;
- strategy concentration across optimizer runs;
- outcome sensitivity to small parameter perturbations;
- automation adoption rate/timing/payback distribution;
- new decisions/capabilities after automation;
- switching/hybridization costs and recovery time;
- late-game time until wealth saturation;
- sink portfolio utilization by persona;
- exploit yield relative to normal routes;
- simulation-to-player-surface disagreement once interactive evidence exists.

Do not create one economy-health or sandbox-health score.

## 18. Bounded experiments

### EPA-E1 — Source/sink lifecycle simulation

Build a bounded abstract economy with representative early/mid/late sources/sinks across several resource classes.

**Pass:** resources have understandable lifecycle/uses and no obvious accidental unbounded accumulation or starvation under reasonable policies.  
**Failure:** revise source/sink/conversion/gating structure before tuning numbers.

### EPA-E2 — Lifestyle viability tournament

Run several persona policies against shared foundational goals plus specialization goals.

**Pass:** several materially different policies progress and sustain goals through distinct mixes of direct production/trade/services/relationships/substitution.  
**Failure:** hidden mandatory path or nominal specialization.

### EPA-E3 — Dominant-route exploit search

Use optimizers/search to maximize several objectives independently and jointly.

**Pass:** no early/general route dominates most objectives with negligible tradeoffs; exploits are detected as anomalies rather than accepted balance.  
**Failure:** inspect source/sink/gates/opportunity cost rather than merely flattening rewards.

### EPA-E4 — Automation payback/burden sweep

For representative chores/systems, sweep scale, attention value, setup cost, maintenance, output, and alternatives.

**Pass:** there exist plausible regions where manual, partial, and stronger automation are each rational for different goals/scales; higher automation frees attention and creates new decisions.  
**Failure:** automation is trivial mandatory, useless, or passive.

### EPA-E5 — Switching/catch-up simulation

Have policies invest deeply in one route then pivot/hybridize at several progression stages.

**Pass:** switching has meaningful cost but can recover to viable new goals without arbitrary permanent account damage; intentional branch exclusions remain signaled.  
**Failure:** early experimentation becomes punitive or switching is costless enough to erase identity.

### EPA-E6 — Inflation/sink long-run simulation

Run mature profiles well beyond basic scarcity resolution.

**Pass:** several sink/ambition families continue consuming/transforming accumulated value for meaningful goals; one currency/resource does not render the rest irrelevant.  
**Failure:** revise sink portfolio/progression layers/production growth.

### EPA-E7 — Progression graph reachability/fuzz

Compile gates/unlocks into a graph/state-search model and inject missing/changed requirements.

**Pass:** required goals are reachable under supported branches, accidental cycles/dead ends are detected, intentional exclusions are explainable.  
**Failure:** gate model/validator insufficient.

### EPA-E8 — Sensitivity/robustness analysis

Perturb key rates/costs within bounded ranges and rerun selected personas.

**Pass:** design conclusions (e.g., multiple viable routes) do not disappear under tiny changes; fragile thresholds are identified for focused tuning.  
**Failure:** economy rests on knife-edge balance and needs structural alternatives.

### EPA-E9 — Simulation/model bias audit

Compare decisions/outcomes from at least a scripted optimizer, bounded rational/noisy persona, and later real gameplay-kernel agent on the same simplified scenario.

**Pass:** model differences are visible/versioned and strong conclusions survive or are narrowed.  
**Failure:** abstract simulator/policy is encoding the desired answer.

## 19. Failure modes and defenses

### Universal-money collapse
**Failure:** best cash route buys/bypasses all systems.  
**Defense:** imperfect fungibility, noncash capabilities/access, route diversity tests.

### Lifestyle theater
**Failure:** all routes secretly require the same production core.  
**Defense:** foundational-need route audit and persona viability tournament.

### Dead currency/resource
**Failure:** output accumulates with no meaningful sinks/uses.  
**Defense:** source/sink lifecycle model and late-stage simulation.

### Sink tax
**Failure:** late-game sink only deletes wealth to extend grind.  
**Defense:** sink must support capabilities/goals/expression/ambition.

### Automation mandatory immediately
**Failure:** fixed setup pays back so quickly that manual play is irrational almost at once.  
**Defense:** payback sweep, setup constraints, scale-dependent leverage, preserve meaningful manual path.

### Automation never rational
**Failure:** setup/maintenance overwhelms benefits.  
**Defense:** scale/attention analysis and higher-order capability value.

### Passive automation end-state
**Failure:** optimal play is waiting for machines.  
**Defense:** automation tiers must open allocation/logistics/expansion/risk/quality decisions.

### Catch-up exploit
**Failure:** repeated respec/refund/switch gives unlimited value or bypasses gates.  
**Defense:** exploit search and explicit switching resource flow.

### Progression cycle
**Failure:** required unlocks mutually depend.  
**Defense:** graph compilation/search and alternative-source validation.

### One optimizer defines balance
**Failure:** economy tuned to scripted profit bot and hurts other play.  
**Defense:** persona population, different goals, model-bias audit, player-surface evidence.

### Fragile knife-edge
**Failure:** tiny numeric change destroys viability.  
**Defense:** sensitivity sweeps and structural substitutes.

### Infinite compounding
**Failure:** production growth trivializes all sinks/content.  
**Defense:** long-run simulation, scaling constraints/sinks/new ambitions, exploit detection.

## 20. Risks and unresolved tensions

- Strong trade can preserve optionality but also let the best money strategy bypass system identity.
- Nonfungible progression protects identity but can become mandatory silo grinding.
- Automation relief can conflict with players who value ritual/manual mastery.
- Broad sandbox viability makes authored pacing and economic tuning harder.
- Dynamic markets could add depth but also volatility/exploits/complexity; not assumed.
- Strong late-game sinks can feel coercive if ordinary play is balanced around maximum-scale wealth.
- Simulation can measure obvious structural problems but may miss emotional pacing, perceived fairness, and intrinsic action quality.

## 21. Open questions

1. Which value dimensions/currencies should be general versus domain-specific?
2. What foundational resources/capabilities must every playthrough obtain, and through how many routes?
3. How much cross-system trade should be allowed before specialization loses identity?
4. Which progression gates can general currency bypass, if any?
5. How should player time/attention be represented in balance tooling without reducing play to efficiency?
6. Which automation systems need maintenance/energy/logistics versus pure setup cost?
7. What scale ranges should make manual/partial/full automation rational?
8. Which late-game ambitions appeal to low-industry/social/exploration/direct-play lifestyles?
9. What switching costs preserve identity without punishing experimentation?
10. How should intentional mutually exclusive branches be represented in reachability tests?
11. Which market dynamics, if any, add meaningful decisions without destabilizing sandbox viability?
12. What persona policies best expose hidden dominance while avoiding a single “correct” player model?
13. What product evidence should determine concrete inflation/wealth-saturation targets?

## 22. Reopen conditions

Reopen this proposal if:

- simulations show a universal route dominates most goals despite proposed structure;
- several intended lifestyles cannot reach foundational needs without constant unwanted system participation;
- automation payback cannot produce meaningful manual/partial/automated regions;
- late-game wealth saturates far faster than meaningful ambitions/sinks can absorb;
- progression graph becomes too complex to validate or author safely;
- switching is either effectively irreversible or so cheap that specialization has no identity;
- persona/model-bias audits show simulation conclusions are policy artifacts;
- executable gameplay evidence contradicts abstract economy assumptions;
- narrative/social/world design requires value/gate forms not captured by the economy/progression model;
- cross-domain review finds economy rules undermine discovery, accessibility, or player agency.
